In 2017, a group unhappy with Bitcoin's 1MB block-size limit hard
forked to a new chain with larger blocks, calling it Bitcoin Cash. Some
argued their version was the "real" Bitcoin, pointing to specific
phrases in Satoshi's original whitepaper. But Bitcoin isn't defined by
what a paper says or who argues loudest online — it's defined by
which software the economically significant nodes (wallets,
exchanges, merchants) actually choose to keep running.

The vast majority of those node operators didn't switch, so the two
chains simply diverged: Bitcoin stayed Bitcoin, and the new chain
became a separate asset with its own price, its own history from the
split point onward, and its own (much smaller) network defending it.
That's the fundamental rule for any hard fork attempted without full
agreement — it doesn't overwrite the original, it just forks off a new
coin.

**What Bitcoin fixes here:** Bitcoin can't be captured by force or by
argument. A disagreement can't overwrite the original chain — the most
it can ever do is start a separate one, and let people choose which of
the two they keep running.

**Analogy:** Like a country splitting when a breakaway region declares
independence — the old country doesn't disappear just because the
new one insists it's the true successor; both simply exist side by side
afterward.
