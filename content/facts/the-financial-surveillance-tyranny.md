Every card swipe, bank transfer, and online payment in the modern
financial system passes through infrastructure built to watch it.
Banks file reports on "suspicious" activity, payment processors can
freeze accounts without a court order, and entire countries have
throttled political dissidents by cutting off their access to
payment rails — not because they broke a law, but because a
gatekeeper decided they shouldn't be allowed to transact.

That's not hypothetical: protesters, journalists, advocacy groups,
and ordinary people mid-dispute have all had bank accounts frozen
or payment processors pull the plug on them, with no trial and
often no real explanation. The infrastructure that carries your
money was never neutral — it's a chokepoint, and chokepoints get
used.

**What Bitcoin fixes here:** A Bitcoin transaction doesn't ask
permission from a bank, a payment processor, or a government before
it settles. There's no central ledger operator who can freeze your
balance or block a transfer because they don't like who you're
paying or why — the rules are enforced by open-source software and
a global network of nodes, not a compliance department.

**Analogy:** Like the difference between handing a letter to a
censor who reads it before deciding whether to deliver it, and
dropping it straight in a mailbox no one controls — the message
goes out because the system was built to carry it, not because
someone approved of its contents.
